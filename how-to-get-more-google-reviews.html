How to get more Google reviews

Updated August 2026 · 6 minute read

Most businesses do not have a review problem. They have an asking problem. Here is what actually works, what to say, and which shortcuts will get your listing into trouble.

Why reviews matter more than most owners think

When someone searches for a business like yours, they rarely see one option. They see a short list, and every business on that list looks broadly similar: a name, a category, a star rating and a review count.

The rating and the count are usually the only points of difference a customer can actually compare before deciding who to call. That is a lot of weight resting on a number most businesses never deliberately manage.

Reviews also feed into how Google ranks businesses in local results. Volume, recency and the fact that people are engaging with your profile all contribute. That said, be sceptical of anyone who promises rankings in exchange for reviews. It is one input among many.

The single biggest lever: ask in person, at the right moment

There is a window, usually a minute or two long, immediately after you have finished doing good work. The customer is pleased, they are looking at you, and they feel a small amount of goodwill they would like to discharge. That is when to ask.

Every hour you wait, that goodwill fades. By the time an automated email arrives on Thursday, the haircut is a memory and the car is just a car again.

In-person asks convert dramatically better than remote ones. If you only change one thing after reading this, change when and how you ask, not which tool you use to ask.

What to actually say

Most staff do not ask because they feel awkward, and they feel awkward because nobody gave them the words. Give them the words.

Short, low pressure, and specific about the mechanism:

  • "If you have got ten seconds, tapping your phone on this puts a review straight up. It really helps us."
  • "Glad you are happy with it. Would you mind leaving us a quick Google review? This card takes you straight there."
  • "We are a small business, so reviews genuinely make a difference for us. No worries at all if you would rather not."

Notice what these have in common. They are honest, they give an easy out, and they explain that it is quick. Nobody is being pressured, which is exactly why it works.

Remove the friction entirely

Even a willing customer will give up if reviewing is fiddly. Asking someone to search for your business, pick you out from similar names and scroll to the review section loses a large share of people who genuinely intended to help.

The fix is a direct link, which you can get from your Google Business Profile, and then put in front of customers in whatever form suits your business: an NFC card they tap, a QR code on a counter stand, a link in a text message, or a line in your email signature.

Build it into the routine, not the hero effort

Review generation fails when it depends on someone remembering. It works when it becomes part of the job, like taking payment.

  • Give every staff member their own card. The person who did the work is the person who should ask.
  • Attach it to an existing step. Payment is the natural moment, because the customer is already stopped.
  • Talk about it. Mention new reviews in team catch-ups. Recognition drives the habit better than instruction.
  • Make it visible. A counter stand reminds staff as much as it invites customers.

Reply to every review

Replies are read by prospective customers far more often than owners assume, and they cost nothing.

For positive reviews, keep it short, specific and human. "Thanks Sam, glad the fade came out how you wanted. See you next time" beats a copied and pasted thank you every time.

For negative reviews, this is where new customers judge you. Reply calmly, do not argue the detail in public, acknowledge the experience and offer to sort it out directly. A measured reply to a bad review reassures readers more than a perfect record does, because it shows what happens when something goes wrong.

Resist the urge to demand removal. Genuine negative reviews rarely get taken down, and the attempt is time you could spend replying well.

What not to do

These are the shortcuts that put your profile at risk. They are common, they are sold as services, and they are not worth it.

  • Review gating. Surveying customers first and only sending the satisfied ones to Google. Explicitly prohibited.
  • Incentives. Discounts, free coffees or prize draws in exchange for reviews. Prohibited, and it distorts what you learn.
  • Buying reviews. They get detected and removed, and can take the profile down with them.
  • Staff and family reviews. Often obvious to readers, and against the rules.
  • Blasting everyone at once. Forty reviews in one week after two years of silence looks exactly as unusual as it is.

A steady trickle beats a burst. Two or three genuine reviews a week, every week, is more valuable and more believable than fifty in a fortnight, and it never puts your listing at risk.

A realistic starting point

If you want a plan you can start this week:

  1. Get your direct Google review link from your Business Profile.
  2. Put it somewhere physically present at the point of payment.
  3. Write one sentence and make sure everyone on the team knows it.
  4. Ask every customer, not just the ones you expect to be happy.
  5. Reply to every review within a couple of days.
  6. Check the count once a month, not once a day.

That is genuinely most of it. One Melbourne barbershop we set this up for reached 16 reviews in the first week of opening a new shop, from a standing start, because the asking was built in from day one rather than added later.
